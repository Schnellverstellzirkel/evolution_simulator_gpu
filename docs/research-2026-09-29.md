## Decision: the morphology reserve

Recommendation: draw reserve parents from the global reserve. Do not add per-island reserves and do not remove the reserve.

What the code does today. `offer_morphology` only runs on the global archive (`src/storage.rs` around line 838), so the reserve lives there. The structural emitter's 10% reserve draw calls `sample_morphology` on the island archive (`src/storage.rs` around line 1493), where no morphology entries exist. It always falls back to `sample_local_competitive`. Reserve creatures never breed.

There is a second bug behind the first. Eviction from a full reserve needs `visits >= MIN_MORPHOLOGY_DESCENDANTS` (8) in `offer_morphology` (`src/qd.rs` around line 719). `visits` only grows through `islands[island].visit(parent_index)` (`src/storage.rs` around line 1662). Global reserve entries never get visits. So once 64 topologies are in, the reserve is frozen. It admits no new body plan again. Only the existing 64 can improve, and only when a child of the same plan beats them. The reserve was meant to hold recent innovations. It now holds the first 64.

Why the global reserve.
- It is the smallest change. Point the draw at `self.archive`, carry an "archive kind" in the plan (the plan reads `entries[index].creature.id`, `protected_until` and `topology` from the parent's archive), and call `self.archive.visit(idx)` for reserve draws so eviction works. The child still lands in its island slot and competes for an island niche there. That is how a reserve lineage gets into the islands.
- The cost is small. Ten percent of the structural share (35%) is 3.5% of offspring.
- Island models get their diversity from isolation plus rare migration (Whitley, Rana and Heckendorn 1999 is the usual reference, not re-read here). Four private reserves would split 64 places into 16 each, and would duplicate the same plan across islands unless each island kept a different plan. That adds migration rules the search does not have today. A shared pool of stepping stones does not disturb island isolation.
- Cheney et al. 2018 protect a new body until its controller has readapted. They do it by age, not by an isolated pool. The reserve is this codebase's version of that protection, and it only works if reserve members get offspring. The three-generation `protected_until` rule protects a plan inside a cell but gives it no offspring. The working reserve gives offspring.
- Removal loses something the code already pays for. Reserve candidates trigger contender checks (`ContenderReason::Reserve`, `src/storage.rs` around line 550). That GPU cost is spent today for creatures that never breed.

Evidence that it is safe. The old benchmark in `docs/search-benchmark.md` (5 seeds, 300 generations, single archive, so the reserve worked there) shows mean best distance 112.8 m against 112.0 m without it, QD score 9,115 against 8,531, distinct behavior-archive topologies 36.6 against 31.6, and 3.8 times more first-time topology admissions. Best distance per seed was mixed (+87 m, -99 m, -6 m, +18 m, +4 m). So it is neutral to mildly positive on the search score and positive on body-plan diversity. That run predates the current physics and the island ring. Confirm with 3 seeds of `search_ab` (reserve draw on against the current no-op) and stop when 3 seeds decide it, as the owner asked.

One caution. Do not change the reserve's admission rule or size in the same push. Fix the draw and the visit count only. If the reserve then dominates parent choice in some seed, the 10% figure is the knob, and it already exists.

## Details: evolution quality

### E1. Reserve fix
Covered above.

### E2. A finer archive for 3M-creature generations
The behavior grid has `BINS = [6, 8, 1, 6, 5]`, which is 1,440 cells per island and 5,760 over four islands (`src/qd.rs` line 10). A generation has 3 million children. That is about 500 children per cell per generation. The archive fills in a few generations and the search then spends most of its evaluations on cells that hold a well-tuned elite. Cells are the unit of diversity here, so the number of cells caps biodiversity.

QDax (Lim, Allard, Grillotti, Cully 2022) shows that MAP-Elites runs with large batches "without any significant effect on the performance", and that runtime falls by two orders of magnitude. The paper's point is about runtime. It does not say that more cells help. The reason to try more cells is the batch arithmetic above. CVT-MAP-Elites (Vassiliades, Chatzilygeroudis, Mouret 2018) exists because a regular grid cannot grow in many dimensions. It partitions the descriptor space into k cells with k-means centroids, so k can be 10,000 or more for a five-axis descriptor. Cell lookup becomes a nearest-centroid search. That costs a few dozen flops per creature on the CPU or in the kernel epilogue. Alternatively add bins to the existing axes. The height axis and the contact axis are already log or linear, so going from 1,440 to about 10,000 per island is a change to `BINS`.

Expected effect: more elites, more distinct gaits, a QD score that scales with cell count by construction (so do not compare QD scores across the change). Risk: every cell gets fewer children per generation, migration moves one tenth of many more elites, and archive memory grows. Test: 3 seeds at 40 generations, compare best distance and the number of distinct body plans in the top 50, not QD score. Bump `qd::VERSION`.

Evidence is indirect. I found no paper that measures cell count against batch size for this case.

### E3. Age-fair comparison and a nursery
The local measurements in `docs/search-research.md` show a new limb starts at 3 to 39% of its parent's distance and does not catch a tuned parent after 256 evaluations. That matches Cheney et al. 2018 (protect innovation until the controller adapts), Mertan and Cheney 2025 (evolution undervalues newly mutated bodies), and the infant-learning results of Luo et al. 2022 and 2023, where learning before selection raises fitness and Lamarckian inheritance helps. de Bruin et al. 2026 report that starting learning from peers with similar bodies beats learning from scratch at equal budget. Song et al. 2026 warn that short learning budgets favor fast learners.

Fit here: a child of a structural or novelty emitter belongs to one plan. Group the new plans of a generation and give each group a short CMA run (the CMA emitters exist, up to 96) before any of them competes. Offer only the best result of the group to the archive. This is a change to the emitter and contender logic in `src/storage.rs` and `src/qd.rs`. It costs evaluations that today go to other children, so measure at equal budget. The reserve fix in this note is the cheap first half of the same idea. Do it first and measure before building the nursery.

### E4. Bandit shares without the floor
Cully 2021 (multi-emitter MAP-Elites) runs several emitter types and lets a bandit choose their shares from recent archive improvement. It improved quality, diversity and convergence speed on six tasks. Fialho et al. 2010 analyze bandit-based operator selection and reward by fitness improvement. Here the shares start at CMA 35%, structural 35%, novelty 30%, immigrant 0% and the local notes say half of each prior is a fixed floor (`src/qd.rs` around line 835). A floor makes the bandit slow to react. Two changes: reward per GPU-second instead of per child (structural children cost more because bodies grow), and lower the floor to 10 to 20% of the prior. Check the current floor value in the code before changing it, because the notes date from before the last two weeks of edits.

### E5. CMA-MAE
Fontaine and Nikolaidis 2023 replace "beat the cell's elite" with "beat the cell's threshold", where the threshold moves toward accepted scores at rate alpha. Their claim is that CMA-ME "prematurely abandons the objective in favor of exploration". The ranking in `src/storage.rs` (search for the CMA-ME improvement ranking near line 858) puts every new cell first, which is the CMA-ME rule. The paper reports state-of-the-art QD score on its benchmarks, which are a linear projection domain, an arm repertoire, and a Hearthstone-style latent space domain. I did not find results on morphology plus control. Weak evidence for this game. It is a contained change in the optimizer ranking. Test with 3 seeds.

### E6. Line variation and crossover
Iso+LineDD (Vassiliades and Mouret 2018) adds isotropic noise plus a random multiple of the difference between two elites. Their tests show a large speedup on a toy function and a redundant arm and a hexapod. Hutchinson et al. 2026 report gains from discrete gene crossover in QD on three locomotion tasks. The code already crosses same-plan elites for 20% of structural and novelty children and groups elites by plan (`by_plan` in `src/storage.rs`). A line operator for the CMA fallback and the local mutation is a few lines: pick a second elite from the same `by_plan` group, add `sigma * (x_j - x_i)`. Same-plan elites share gene layout, so the difference is defined. Cheap to try.

### E7. Curriculum across worlds
POET (Wang, Lehman, Clune, Stanley 2019) and Enhanced POET (2020) show that letting solutions move between environments finds solutions that direct optimization in the hard environment does not. Here the player toggles effects. On a world change the code re-tests the global elites and clears islands, emitter stats and CMA state. A cheaper step-stone rule: after a change keep the previous world's top elites as immigrants in the islands for a few generations (immigrant share is 0% except after a change, per the notes, so part of this exists). I do not see a need for a POET-style automatic environment generator. The owner wants effects as buttons.

### E8. The `mutability` gene
It is mutated and never read. Either multiply the mutation scale by it (a step-size self-adaptation, as in classic evolution strategies) or delete it. Deleting saves genome bytes. The self-adaptation literature is old and mostly on continuous problems, so I do not expect a large effect. Do whichever is cheaper and record which.

### E9. Stall extinctions
Lehman and Miikkulainen 2015 show extinction events can raise evolvability and speed later progress in their setups. The game has an Extinction button. An automatic per-island version (when an island's best has not moved for N generations, drop its bottom half) is small. The four-island ring already refills empty islands with immigrants. Low priority because the owner controls it by hand.

### E10. Novelty step
Local measurement: novelty children at scale 0.75 keep a median 6 to 20% of the parent's distance and 31 to 43% fall. Novelty search (Lehman and Stanley 2011) needs offspring that behave differently, not offspring that are broken. `src/evolution.rs` line 1517 sets the scale (0.75, and 2.25 in 5% of cases). Try 0.25 to 0.4. Note that anatomy operators changed the offspring mix since that measurement. Re-measure with `mutation_audit`.

### E11 and E12. Not recommended now
Deep grids (Flageat and Cully 2020) handle noisy scores by keeping several candidates per cell. The contender check already discards lucky scores, so the gain is small. Learned descriptors (AURORA, Grillotti and Cully 2022) need a trajectory encoder and lose the readable archive map the owner uses. Neural controllers, generative encodings (Hornby and Pollack 2002; Cheney et al. 2013) and NEAT speciation (Stanley and Miikkulainen 2002) are large changes. Evolution Gym (Bhatia et al. 2021) is a caution: all tested co-design algorithms failed on its hardest tasks, so more machinery in the encoding does not guarantee better walkers. Controller distillation (Mertan and Cheney 2025) targets migration between bodies and would fit the island ring, but I found no result on planar bone and muscle creatures.

### About walkers
The owner sees no walkers in v1 or v2. Most of the evidence in this note is about search. The v2 notes say the winners are compact hoppers that sit under the head-below-neck rule, with many strong muscles. That points at physics and rules more than at the search. Levers already listed in `docs/physics-v2.md` (muscle power scaled to size, tendons, joint damping) are the right place to look. The literature above cannot fix a fitness landscape whose best solutions are hoppers.

## Details: throughput

### T1. Overlap the CPU stages with the GPU
The worker is serial: evaluate, archive, breed, repeat, and the GPU is busy 47% of the time. Breeding for generation g+1 needs archive state, but MAP-Elites does not need the newest archive. QDax's result that huge batches do not hurt quality supports breeding batch g+1 from the archive as of g-1 (one batch deep). Then GPU work for g overlaps with archive insertion for g-1 and breeding for g+1. If the CPU stages stay at 4.5 s and the GPU at about 4.5 s, the generation is near 5 s, a rate near 600k creatures/s. The rate is near 3M / 5 s. To go beyond that the CPU stages must shrink (T2, T3).

Needed care: the world-change and load paths (drain the pipeline), determinism (the owner wants deterministic search, so the staleness must be fixed at one batch and the order of archive updates fixed), and the reserve draw (uses the archive that was current when the plan was made).

### T2. Filter on the device
Today all 3M results go to the host, and the host inserts them. Almost all are rejected: the archive holds a few thousand elites, and a creature enters only if it beats the elite of its cell. Upload the elite fitness per cell (island cells and global cells, about 6k floats) at the start of each batch. In the kernel epilogue compute the cell index from the descriptors, compare the score with the threshold, and append the creature index to a contender list with an atomic counter if it passes. Return only the list and the scores. For ties and determinism, sort the list by index on the host, or use a packed 64-bit key (ordered float bits high, index low) with `atomicMax` per cell.

This shrinks the archive stage from work proportional to 3M to work proportional to the contender count, and the fine check already runs on that subset. It also cuts PCIe traffic for results. The screen threshold is the same kind of device-side test and already exists, so the pattern is in the code.

Limit: descriptors for the reserve and for CMA feedback need every sample's rank, not only contenders (`from_optimizer` in `src/storage.rs` explains why all optimizer samples get ranked). Return the optimizer samples in full and filter the rest.

### T3. Materialize children on the device
A child is a copy of a parent genome plus noise. About 3M children of about 500 bytes each is 1.5 GB written by the CPU per generation. CMA (35%) and novelty (30%) children are dominated by Gaussian noise on a parent's genes. Send a plan record (parent id, operator id, scale, counter) and let the kernel prologue build the child from the parent genome that already sits on the device. This is the plan record in `docs/data-architecture.md` section 5.2. Counter-based generators such as Philox (Salmon et al., SC 2011, cited from memory, not fetched here) give each child a reproducible stream without shared state, which keeps the search deterministic. Structural and anatomy operators change the topology and use `repair`, so they stay on the CPU. CMA sampling with a diagonal covariance is `mean + sigma * d * z`, which is easy on the device. Expected: the CPU breeding wall drops by roughly the local-mutation share of its time. Large change, so do T1 and T2 first.

EvoJAX (Tang, Tian, Ha 2022), evosax (Lange 2022) and EvoX (Huang et al. 2023) all put the strategy update, variation and evaluation on the accelerator. None of them handles topology-changing variation with repair. So the partial split above is the realistic version.

### T4. The v2 contact solve
What `docs/physics-v2.md` says today: after the step without the ground, up to four nodes that would touch get contacts. A contact-space matrix is built and solved by projected Gauss-Seidel, 8 warm-started sweeps plus 4 planting sweeps, and the step is then repeated once with each contact's velocity measured in the end pose. Rates on the RTX 4060, 20,000 bodies: 8 contacts with 20 cold sweeps 4.1M creature-steps/s, 4 contacts with 8 warm and 4 planting sweeps 22M, 1 contact with 1 sweep 62M, no ground contact 97M. The kernel uses 168 registers, so 12 warps fit per SM.

I did not read the kernel. The suggestions come from the doc and the sources.

1. Substeps with one sweep and no warm start. Macklin et al. 2019 ("Small steps in physics simulation") show that n substeps with one solver iteration each beat one step with n iterations, with less constraint error and damping. Catto's Solver2D (2024) reaches the same result for Box2D's Soft Step solver: "As the step size gets smaller, the approximation improves and non-linearities fade away", and Soft Step uses no warm starting and defaults to 4 substeps. PhysX's TGS solver makes the same trade (its docs say TGS often needs no velocity iterations). For this kernel: two or four substeps of the contact part per 60 Hz step, one sweep each, contact set fixed at the step start. The muscle and joint dynamics can stay at step rate. This cuts sweeps from 12 to 2 to 4. Risk: it changes contact behavior, and `docs/physics-v2.md` lists many exploits found by evolution (kick sled, joint limit energy, planting in the start pose). Every change needs `first_generation` and the strobe ledgers again.
2. Build the contact matrix once per step, cheaply. With at most 4 contacts on a tree of 8 or fewer bones, the matrix is `J M^-1 J^T` for 4 contact points. If it is built by one articulated-body pass per contact direction (8 passes for normal and tangent), that is the dominant cost. Featherstone's book (2008) and Carpentier, Budhiraja and Mansard (RSS 2021) give operational-space inverse inertia computed from the tree's sparse structure in one backward and one forward pass over the joint-space inverse inertia, then a small product. Baraff 1996 gives linear-time constrained dynamics on loop-free constraint graphs. Check first how the current kernel builds it. If it uses per-contact passes, the shared pass is an O(n^2) once-per-step cost against O(k n) per contact.
3. Cap contacts at two or three normal contacts for bodies whose extra nodes are in the air, and keep the rest as position pushes. The v2 doc says nodes left out of the solve sink and are pushed out and cause resting jitter (88 J of unpaid energy in the median plank), so this needs the ledger, not just the rate.
4. Jacobi with mass splitting (Tonge, Benevolenski and Voroshilov 2012) exists to parallelize over contacts. Here each creature is one lane and the contacts belong to one lane, so Gauss-Seidel order is free and Jacobi gives nothing. Not recommended.
5. Registers. The measured fact in this repo is that above 128 registers the SM runs 12 resident warps instead of 16. Try `-maxrregcount=128` (or `__launch_bounds__`) for the CUDA build first. The L2 rate measured here is high (1.6 TB/s), so a modest spill may cost less than the lost occupancy. This is a one-line experiment. If it loses, split off the rare paths (fine-check settings, terrain effects) so they stop reserving registers for the hot loop.

Honest note: v2 is at 0.27x of v1 per step and did not produce a walker in the same-run comparison. Even at 2x of v1's cost, the case to switch depends on the levers in section E "About walkers", not on solver speed. Getting to 0.5x is worth attempting through items 1 and 2 and then judging by one lever run.

### T5. Register cap on v1
See T4 item 5. The v1 kernel uses about 120 registers by the older notes and more at 7 or more nodes, so the cap may already matter for v1. Measure with the same 100k creature benchmark used for v2 (`eval-bench`).

### T6. Compaction
Aila and Laine 2009 introduced persistent threads with dynamic fetch for ray tracing. The repo tried persistent lanes (`claude/lanes`) and got 148k against 244k creatures/s because the kernel used 155 to 168 registers and nothing compacted sparse warps. The screen at 5 s and segments that drop fallen creatures already capture much of the gain. I would not retry before the register fix.

### Things not worth doing
Brax (Freeman et al. 2021), MuJoCo XLA and MuJoCo Warp, and Isaac Gym (Makoviychuk et al. 2021) are general engines. They reach millions of steps per second on simple scenes, and MuJoCo Warp reports about 287k steps per second on one benchmark scene with thousands of worlds (a GitHub-hosted figure I did not verify against a paper). This project already runs about 150M creature-steps per second in v1. Those engines confirm the design (one world per lane, all on the device) and give no code to reuse. The MuJoCo docs say the Newton solver "delivers excellent convergence with very few (often just one) solver iterations, and performs well on GPU", and that GPUs prefer dense matrices when they fit. That matches keeping a small dense contact matrix and taking few sweeps.

## Suggested order of work
1. Reserve fix, 3-seed check.
2. T1 pipeline and T2 device filter (each on its own branch, push each when it runs).
3. E2 finer archive, E4 bandit floor and E6 line variation, each a 3-seed A/B at equal wall time.
4. T4 items 1, 2 and 5 on `claude/physics2`.
5. E3 nursery and T3 device-side children after the above.
