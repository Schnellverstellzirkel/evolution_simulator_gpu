# Design decisions

What the game does in search, physics and speed, and the number that decided each choice. Each item stays true until a direct measurement of a change shows the game is better or faster without it. Ideas that lost are in `docs/rejected-ideas.md`.

## Search

Fitness is horizontal center-of-mass distance and nothing else. Behavior (ground contact, cadence, body height, lifted feet) picks the archive niche and never changes the score. The physics rules that end a trial (a fall, a broken joint, head shaking above 8 g) keep distance the only objective.

The search is MAP-Elites with four emitters: CMA tuning, anatomy mutations, novelty and random immigrants. Immigrants only seed empty archives. Each island also keeps a nursery: 10% of its slots hold new random bodies and the bodies bred from them, which compete only against each other for 10 generations. Then the survivors enter the island archive and compete on distance alone. The emitter shares stay at the 35% CMA, 35% structural, 30% novelty prior. A reward-following bandit tied with the fixed shares.

Four islands are fully isolated and a fifth hub island receives copies of each isolated island's fastest tenth every 25 generations. Islands that exchanged their best tenth every 5 generations converged on one design within about 15 generations. At 241 generations one seed reached 847 m with migration every 5 generations, 1,243 m with none and 1,556 m every 25.

Each archive starts with one cell per way of moving (6 ground contact, 8 cadence, 6 body height on a log scale and 5 feet, 1,440 cells) and keeps its fastest creature there. Body height is logarithmic so small and giant bodies do not share one cell. When an archive reaches its plateau (it covers three quarters of the ways of moving, and either half of its elites are within 1% of its best distance or its best has not risen for 30 generations) it is refined: each way of moving gets a cell for each body class, 2 shapes by the start pose's width over height (compact under 1.5, long from 1.5) times 2 sizes by node count (small up to 10, large 11 and more), 5,760 cells, and every elite moves to its class's cell. A nursery never refines, and a world change keeps the layout of every archive, so the elites tested again in the new world keep the cells of their body classes. A 64-entry morphology reserve per island gives new body plans offspring without counting toward coverage or QD score. In a 5-seed run the reserve admitted 3.8 times as many first-time body plans at equal best distance (112.8 m against 112.0 m).

The classes exist because evolved archives are plateaus. At generation 590 of the owner's game 249 of the 1,440 elites tied the best distance within a millimetre, 1,297 were within 1% of it, and the 400 fastest elites replayed on the GPU to distances within 2 mm of each other. The top distance belongs to the world and many bodies reach it, so the first body to reach it kept its cell and its relatives took the neighbors: the largest clade held 766 of one island's 1,370 cells, 891 body plans filled the 1,440 cells of the global archive, and the 36 effective clades of the owner's save at generation 1,512 were 96 clades in 1,431 cells. A new body plan cannot win a cell on distance there, and one elite per way of moving cannot keep two designs. The classes give each kind of body its own cell. On the owner's save at generation 1,512 (a plateau) continued for 20 generations at 500k, in two runs side by side, the best stayed 34.07 m and the movement-grid QD 42.6k with and without the classes (42,613 and 42,596). The five islands and the global archive refined, cells went from 1,431 to 5,413, body plans from 907 to 3,378, body types (node and muscle counts) from 344 to 707, effective clades from 36 to 77 (280 clades against 93) and the elites within 1% of the best from 987 to 1,850. A shape class and a size class of 3 each (12,960 cells) held the same best distance and QD there, with 6,860 plans and 104 effective clades, but the save was 176 MB against 100 MB. One class axis alone (3 sizes or 3 shapes) gave fewer clades.

The classes start at the plateau because a climbing archive of 5,760 cells spreads its children over many weak elites that cannot be replaced until a body of their class beats them, and the best lineage no longer floods every cell. Measured from the first generation, the best distance differed from the old grid by -16% to +15% over five runs, which is the spread between trajectories of one seed (any change of the archive starts a different one): 500k creatures per generation for 60 generations on seeds 38 and 39 (41.8 and 42.5 m against 39.4 and 42.1 m), 1M for 40 generations on seeds 41 and 42 (34.3 and 45.8 m against 40.0 and 39.8 m) and 3M for 20 generations on seed 38 (35.3 m against 41.9 m). The movement-grid QD was level (22.0k against 21.5k on average), and the classes gave 2.0 to 4.1 times as many body plans and 1.5 to 2.2 times as many effective clades (231 against 140 at 500k, 306 and 325 against 188 and 140 at 1M). The runs cannot show whether the climb is slower, so a climbing archive is the old archive. With the plateau rule, 1M creatures for 40 generations on seeds 41 and 42 gave the old archive's result to every digit (40.0 and 39.8 m, QD 21.1k and 22.2k, 668 and 558 body plans), because no archive had reached its plateau. Two runs of one seed at 40k creatures also differ on this machine (`docs/backlog.md`). The share of elites near the best is not the only sign of a plateau. In the owner's save at generation 1,512 islands 0, 1 and 3 held 25% to 39% of their elites within 1% of their best, but their bests had stood for 96, 109 and 81 generations. So an archive that covers three quarters of the ways of moving is also refined when its best has stood for 30 generations, which is the island optimizer's own stall. Ties at the best distance replace the incumbent only by noise below 1e-5 m, and the archive hardly changes at the plateau (4 elites of 1,431 changed per generation in the owner's save), so a tie margin does nothing.

A world change keeps the layout of a refined archive. Starting again with one elite per way of moving threw away the body classes of an evolved archive at every button press and every autochange step: an island of 5,760 elites kept at most 1,440 when its elites were tested again. The archive refills with evolved bodies, so spreading them over classes costs no climb. On the owner's save (500k creatures per generation, all five islands refined), 14 generations after the next autochange step of its schedule (mud from dry to damp) the archives held 5,557 cells, 3,718 body plans and 1,276 clades (effective 536) against 1,424 cells, 951 plans and 339 clades (165) with a coarse restart. The best distance in the new world was 32.43 m against 33.00 m and the movement-grid QD 39.8k against 38.9k. A slope of 8% gave 5,402 cells, 3,471 plans, 1,503 clades (738), 33.65 m and 35.6k against 1,421 cells, 955 plans, 450 clades (246), 33.17 m and 35.6k. The best distances differ by -1.7% and +1.4%, which is the noise of one run. Archive seconds per generation after the change (median of 11 generations) were 0.36 s and 0.52 s against 0.24 s and 0.31 s, and breeding 0.56 s in both runs. In the CPU chain at 3M the same change cost 0.80 s of archive and 2.24 s of breeding per generation, against 0.87 s and 2.41 s with the coarse restart (its stand-in scores refill a plateau, so that restart refined again at once). A save written before the first elite re-enters tells its layout by the elites it holds, so it forgets the layout.

Every muscle shares one body clock. This gained 32% best distance on every seed in the first test and was the only change that made bodies of 8 or more nodes competitive (the best such body went from 34 m to 62 m).

The CMA emitter explores in normalized units with a large step (sigma 0.12). Each island also runs a separable CMA-ES in physical units on its fastest body plan. That optimizer needed three things to help: every sample got the same check trial (the fine checks are gone, see below), it stays on its body plan instead of chasing each new record, and it starts at half the base step. With them one seed went from 455 m to 958 m at 241 generations. A uniform sigma of 0.03 for the exploring emitter lost 40%.

Whole-body rescaling is a structural mutation, so evolution can grow bodies from 0.25 m bones to giants. Together with the log height axis it took one seed from 304 m to 856 m.

There are 57 anatomy operators on top of the 7 classic ones (`docs/anatomy-operators.md`). The first 30 gained 21 to 26% best distance and 16 to 28% QD over 10 seeds at equal evaluations, and the top 50 carried 28% fewer muscles. The owner wants more operator types and never fewer.

Cross-plan crossover grafts a limb with its muscles and rhythm from an elite of another body plan on the same island. Over 18 seeds it gave QD x1.25 and best distance x1.08.

Early screening stops a standard trial at 5 s when the creature is below the bar, the 5 s distance the top 10% reached. Screened creatures enter no archive. The 5 s distance keeps every creature of the final top 1% and 96% of the final top 10% (Spearman 0.887). At equal time screening gives 56% more best distance and 2.4 times the QD, at a cost of 14% QD at equal evaluations. Letting screened creatures open empty cells tied at equal time and lost per evaluation.

Standard results are final. Only a creature that would set a new record of its island or nursery gets a confirmation trial from the same pose at four times the standard rate, and it keeps the lower distance. The fine checks it replaced (every archive contender, a perturbed pose, four times the rate) took 40 to 45% of GPU time and needed contender rules, cell claims, check sharing and a 12-block decision lag. An earlier measurement found that a check at twice the rate let integrator exploits through: the top 50 kept a median 12% of their distance from an unseen pose against 44% with the four-times check (`search_ab` prints this share).

Trials last 20 s and physics runs at 60 Hz. Elites evolved at 30 Hz kept a median 38% of their distance when replayed at 60 Hz, against 90% for elites evolved at 60 Hz and replayed at 120 Hz.

The search is deterministic for a fixed seed on one GPU. Blocks are absorbed in ring order and bred only at absorption, and each block keeps the settings it was bred with.

## Scoring and replays

The GPU score is final. A replay is recorded by the scoring kernel with a frame output on a slot and queue of its own, so its result is bit for bit the archive score (`gpu_replays_show_the_gpu_score`).

A GPU that fails is reopened and its unfinished units run again with the same creatures, so they give the same results (`a_lost_gpu_is_reopened_and_gives_the_same_results`). A GPU that does not reopen stops evolution. A GPU out of memory keeps its unit, frees idle buffers and retries with fewer units in flight. Buffers above 1 MiB get 25% headroom, which cut peak GPU memory 15%.

Saves hold only the configuration, history, archives, CMA and emitter state, lineage and queued elites. A 3M game saves 3.4 MB in 0.06 s where the full population took 1,389 MB and 24 s. Loading breeds the ring from the archives. Autosave is off. An older `qd::VERSION` is turned down by the header before the load starts, except version 53, whose archives load with each elite placed in its cell from its stored descriptor (`qd::OLDEST_LOADABLE`). The lineage of a living elite is saved without its creature, because the archive holds it and loading puts it back, and only the global archive's elites and each island's fastest 10 keep their ancestors, as far back as the lineage tab reads (400). The owner's save at generation 1,512 went from 104.2 MB to 44.8 MB, and it is 100 MB after 20 more generations in which its archives refine (43 MB with the old layout).

## Physics

The physics is a planar articulated tree in reduced coordinates (`docs/physics.md`). Bones stay rigid by construction, so the projection passes, the rebuild and the lift that a point-mass chain needs are gone. Evolution finds every hole in a physics model, and each rule below closes one:

- Hill's force-velocity relation bounds muscle power. Without it the best creature threw itself 19 m in one throw.
- Contacts are solved together at velocity level, and friction can only oppose the slip. Solved one at a time, sticking contacts carried the load of sliding ones and nodes reached 30 m/s.
- The spin cap is 15 rad/s. At 40 rad/s a short end bone whipped into the ground and 2,887 of 5,761 N s of friction pushed a node along its own slip (the kick sled, 408 m). At 15 rad/s the same body went 0.8 m.
- Joint limits are inelastic stops. A spring limit let a joint pass unopposed and return 25 kJ against 4.9 kJ of muscle work.
- Feet are planted against the end pose of the step. Planting against the start pose left 42% of an elite's friction pushing along its slip. Now it is 0.0 N s.
- Muscle force and energy scale with the mass a muscle drives. Before, a 100 N muscle drove a 0.05 kg limb at 2,000 m/s^2 and one elite gained 338 J against 26 J of muscle drain. With the scaling it gains 9 J.
- Only active contraction is charged to the energy store. Charging the damper and stretched muscles too tied within the noise but paid for work no muscle did.
- Bones feel air drag. Tendons store and return energy. With tendons QD was 13,162 against 6,437 and 12% of the muscles of sampled elites carried one.
- The contact solve keeps the 4 deepest contacts per step. Eight contacts with cold sweeps ran 4.1M creature-steps/s, four with warm sweeps and planting rounds ran 22M and one ran 62M.

The CUDA kernel is the only physics. The game is NVIDIA-only, and nothing else simulates creatures, so there is one implementation to change and no engines to keep in agreement.

## Speed

The game runs CUDA (`src/cuda_engine.rs`). It was 1.8 times the Vulkan kernel it replaced on an evolved population and equal on a fresh one. The kernels compile without a register cap, which was 3% faster than a 128 cap.

Kernels compile per world (the effects that are on), lane class, recording and fine physics. A world change used to stall the engine thread for 2 to 5 s per press, 52.8 s over 29 presses at 400k creatures, because the fine and recording kernels compiled on first use and `cuModuleLoadData` waits for the kernels running on the GPU (0.1 to 0.9 s on the engine thread, even from the disk cache). Now background threads compile and load every kernel of the current world (three at a time: scoring, then fine, then recording) and one thread at nice 19 does the same for every world one effect level away, scoring first. The engine adopts what is loaded. Revisited worlds and neighbours that finished wait 0 s. A neighbour pressed before its kernels finished waited 0.8 and 1.3 s, and a jump of several effects (a preset) 1.0 to 1.5 s. The disk cache keeps the 200 newest kernels, and a hit touches its file. The status line says "Compiling the new world" once the wait passes 0.3 s. `kernel_wait_seconds` in the stage log is the engine thread's wait.

Work units of 1 s gave 186,700 creatures/s end to end against 168,600 for 3 s units, with 2.5 GB less peak memory. The worker keeps 8 general threads. Adding 8 CPU evaluation workers beside the GPU lowered the rate from 137,800 to 121,300 creatures/s, before the CPU engine was deleted. Archive refreshes recompute only the cells near a change, which cut the archive stage from 3.4 s to 2.1 s per generation at 3M. The archive keeps its cells in a dense table, its body plans as 64-bit keys (crossover partners, island reserves and CMA emitter slots no longer hash topologies), its neighbor scores in packed rows, and it picks the fastest 512 and the 32 least visited elites without sorting all of them. Archive plus breeding wall seconds per generation at 3M on the owner's save, CPU only with stand-in scores (generations 1,515 to 1,518, two runs each): 5.32 and 5.77 s for the code before the classes, 4.14 and 3.64 s with the same layout and these changes, 4.38 and 3.81 s with the classes. Process CPU seconds per generation: 23.9 and 24.6, 21.5 and 20.2, 22.2 and 20.1 (`examples/archive_bench.rs`). With the plateau rule the archives start coarse and refine in the first two generations (generations 1,512 to 1,515): 4.08 s and 21.1 CPU s. Breeding packs children in batches, and the CPU chain is now under a quarter of a generation, so the GPU is the wall.

The game holds a ring of creatures in flight, not a resident 3M population with a spare arena, which reached 23 GB. Each block is one engine unit handed over without a copy. The ring's shape is an experiment constant (`storage::RingShape`), chosen when a game starts, saved with it and written into every generation's statistics. A shape that followed the measured rate would change a seed's search, so it never changes while a game runs. Today the shape is 8 blocks of 196,608 (1,572,864 creatures in flight): blocks wait on confirmation round trips of 1 to 6 s, and at 3M per generation 8 blocks ran 336k to 507k creatures/s in generations 1 to 10 against 155k to 424k for 4 blocks (peak RSS 6.2 GB). The sizing rule is in the code and takes over by itself once the host time per block measures at 0.2 s or less: a block of 50 ms of GPU work (32k to 256k), and a ring of 5 host times per block or the boundary plus 2 blocks, between 0.3 s and 1 s. It is off because a block asks for about 40 confirmation trials (8 per archive, mostly for the nurseries) whatever its size, so a short ring starves the GPU until confirmations have their own slots. 3M creatures in blocks of 32k asked 800 to 3,900 confirmations per generation against 400 to 1,200 in blocks of 196k, and the p95 host time per block was 1.6 to 3.4 s against a ring of 0.9 s. Side by side on a shared GPU (worker_rate at 3M, generations 3 to 7, 2026-10-01) the old ring gave 43.0k creatures/s and 5 blocks of 32k gave 17.0k, with up to 24 s of GPU idle time per generation. With the short ring a world change threw away at most 163,840 creatures, and two runs of one seed gave one statistics digest over 10 generations. At 1M creatures per generation `worker_rate` peaked at 5.2 GB RSS, and the ring does not grow with the generation size. Long sessions grow bodies, and every creature then costs more. Autosave is off by default.

A ring block breeds into its own gene arena. When a block is absorbed, its `Population` is bred again in place (`Population::breed`): every run of 4,096 children gets a part of the arena sized from what the same positions held last time plus a quarter, and each child is written there once, as it is bred. There is no per-child allocation and no second copy of the genes, and breeding one block into a reused arena makes 0.0002 allocations per child (`breed_bench`). The pack writes into registered host buffers (`HostVec`, `cuMemHostRegister` once) that the GPU copies from, so the per-slot staging copy is gone, and a buffer that outgrows its mapping is extended with `mremap` instead of mapped and faulted in again. The global allocator (`src/block_alloc.rs`) keeps freed blocks of 2 MiB and more for the next block of their size. At 1M creatures per generation `worker_rate` went from 450,000 minor page faults per generation to 50,000 (generations 3 to 8), and 5,000 to 15,000 of those are memory that was given back and faulted in again (the rest is the resident set growing as bodies grow). Breeding went from 0.51 to 0.45 s per generation and the rate did not change. Peak RSS rose from 2.3 GB to about 3 GB. Transparent huge pages cut the faults to a few thousand, but compaction stalled single generations for 2 to 5 s on this machine, so they are not used. Page faults per generation are not under 1,000 while bodies grow, because new arena and lineage memory is new memory.

A block's confirmations used to need two to four rounds. The first goes out when the block's standard results are in and comes back in 0.3 s, but it holds at most 512 candidates per archive, and the rest could only be asked at the front of the ring, one round at a time, because blocks are absorbed in order. On the owner's generation 1,510 save about 2,000 candidates per block tie the archives' 34.07 m plateau and every one fails its fine trial, 33k per generation. A follow-up round took 1.3 s to reach the GPU and come back, and the absorption and breeding after it 0.45 s, so a block cost 1.8 s of host time against 1.0 s of GPU time. The engine ran no unit 24.5% of the time and the game ran 116k and 122k creatures/s. A verdict now remembers how many confirmation results it read per archive (decaying by a quarter per block, not saved) and the next block asks for 512 plus twice that in its first round. When results are in and none raised the record, a later round asks for four times as many as have failed. What a verdict decides does not change. Then 92 of 97 blocks needed one round, the engine was idle 4.6% of the time, and the game ran 171k and 173k creatures/s (four runs alternating the two builds on one save, exclusive GPU).

A creature costs about as much per step as the lanes it holds, so a body runs on the fewest lanes that hold its nodes and its muscles (4, 8, 16 or 32). Evolved bodies grow muscles faster than nodes: on the same save 21% of the ring had 16 nodes or fewer and 65 to 96 muscles (13 nodes and 76 muscles on average) and ran on 32 lanes with half the lanes idle. The 16-lane class now runs 6 muscle rounds (96 muscles) and the other classes 4, so only bodies of 17 nodes or more use 32 lanes: 4% of the creatures and 9% of the kernel time. Shared memory for the per-muscle state is what limits the other classes (24,192 B per block on 16 lanes, 4 blocks per multiprocessor). The 6-round kernel spilled more registers (158 B of spill stores against 124 B) until the loop over muscle rounds was rolled (`#pragma unroll 1`). Together the two changes cut kernel time 12% on 100k ring creatures (ncu, 478.8 ms to 420.7 ms), and a body that moves from 32 to 16 lanes costs 66% of its instructions, not the 45% a model of lane counts gives.

The muscle forces of a round reach the bones through shared memory. Evolved hubs carry 20 to 40 muscle ends over a few rounds, so every lane used to run as many slots as the busiest bone needed (about 12 per round where the average bone has 3), and that loop was 13% of the instructions and 18% of the stall samples on 16 lanes. A lane now adds up at most 4 ends of one bone, the other chunks of a hub go to lanes that carry no ends that round, and the hub's lane adds their sums with shuffles (`warp_kernel::plan_round`). That cut kernel time another 5% (ncu, 100k ring creatures, 420.7 ms to 399.4 ms).

Confirmation trials have a slot of their own on each GPU. They used to share the 4 slots of standard units, so a confirmation unit (about 2,300 creatures of 4,800 steps) waited for a slot to free up and then ran beside three standard units. It now goes to a fifth slot first, on a stream of the highest priority, and replays keep theirs. A stream cannot preempt a running block, so the confirmation kernel starts when blocks of the standard kernels exit: its round trip is 3.6 to 4.3 s at the median with 4 standard slots and 1.1 s with 2. A block asks for its confirmations as soon as its results are in and its turn at the front comes 8 blocks later, so either wait hides behind the other blocks and the engine never ran empty in the trace of any run. On the owner's generation 1,510 save (3M per generation, seed 38, generations 1,515 to 1,518, exclusive GPU, two runs each) 4 standard slots gave 190.2k and 186.1k creatures/s without the confirmation slot against 198.6k and 198.5k with it (+5.5% on the means), and 2 standard slots gave 179.0k and 177.7k against 195.9k and 191.2k. Four slots stay: two hold 3.1 GB of GPU memory and 10.2 GB of host memory against 6.0 GB and 13.0 GB for four, and they are 2.5% slower with the confirmation slot. The statistics digest is the same in all ten runs, so the slot layout does not change the search.
