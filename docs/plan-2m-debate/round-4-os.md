# Round 4, OS domain: red team on power, sustained rate, faults, frames and disk

## Attack on K: the joules are in issue and registers, not in L2, and the muscle slope is not held (item 8)

K's muscle record traffic of 0.7 to 0.8 TB/s through L2 sounds expensive and is not: 0.75 TB/s is 23 G sectors of 32 B per second, at roughly 75 pJ per sector at the SM that is under 2 W of the 100. The joules of K are in issued warp instructions and register-file traffic, so my calibration rows 11 and 12 decide K's budget and nothing about its memory traffic does. That is the defence of K's memory design and the attack on its rate claim at once: K's 365 to 610M at W = 2 needs the 45% issue-active at 16 warps that nobody has measured, and under the cap issue-active is bounded by energy, not by scheduling. If rows 11 and 12 give 1.0 nJ per warp instruction for K's mix (about 10% MIO after the shuffles go), K's issue ceiling at 100 W is 80 G warp instructions/s, 33% at 2.49 GHz, and the table drops one row. The number K needs: under 0.45 nJ per warp instruction at its mix.

Muscles. K's step is 50 to 60% muscles at 19 muscles, so the cost per creature-step is about 0.45c + 0.029c per muscle. Nothing in K holds the count; the body cap in P holds nodes (largest elite plus two per island), and muscles grow without nodes: the generation-51 elites sit at 8.6 nodes and 19 muscles, the p90 at 11 nodes and 28 muscles, so muscles per node already run from 2.2 to 2.5. If the mean goes to 25 and the p90 to 40 by generation 100, the mean creature-step costs 1.17c and the p90 1.61c, and the node drift (mean 8.7 to about 10) pushes a third of the population from W = 2 into W = 4 at half the rate. My estimate of K's rate at generation 100 with P's node cap and no muscle cap: 0.65 to 0.72 of its generation-51 rate, so every row of the chair's table loses 30 to 35% by generation 100. It is the old build's slope (4x by generation 30) shifted and flattened, because muscle mass now holds elites but not offspring, and the slope is linear in muscles at 2.9% per muscle. Detection: the stage log already prints mean nodes; it must also print mean and p90 muscles per generation, and size_report at every 10 generations. The number at which it bites: mean muscles above 24. What has to change: the body cap counts muscles as well as nodes (largest elite's muscles plus four per island), or physics prices a muscle in the step (it already does through mass; a slack-length cost in the energy store is the other lever, and it is the owner's).

## Attack on P: the host tax is 21%, not 7 to 9%, until a root setting or a pacing change exists

P's 3.1 to 3.6 busy cores at 7 to 9% assumes those cores run at an efficient clock. On this laptop with EPP performance they run at 5 GHz on any runnable thread, 3.5 cores draw about 30 W of package power, which is the whole 15 W Dynamic Boost swing, and the measured point is 21% at 8 threads. Until rows 1 to 3 exist, the honest host tax for P is 15 to 21%, which takes the chair's 2.0 to 3.0M/s row to 1.6 to 2.5M/s and the 0.87 to 1.3M/s row to 0.7 to 1.1M/s. The number P needs: rows 2 and 3 (4 and 8 threads) at 9% or less. The two ways to get there are both outside cpu's code: the owner's cpufreq cap (rows 5 and 6), or my round-3 burst pacing (row 19a) if the controller is utilization-keyed. The decision belongs to cpu after the rows exist, and until then every table should carry the tax at 15%, not 8.

Second attack on P, the spin. A persistent kernel that waits for refs by spinning on a host-mapped word is a PCIe read per iteration per spinning lane. 288 warps each issuing a 1.5 us load is 190M PCIe reads per second against a link that carries about 10M small transactions per second; the link saturates, the results ring and the gene reads stall behind it, and the GPU draws 40 to 60 W doing nothing while the limiter counts it as load. The rule: one lane per block spins with __nanosleep of 20 to 50 us between reads, the other warps wait at a barrier, and the spin starts only after the block's local queue is empty. Detection: nvidia-smi power above 30 W with utilization near zero during a host stall. Cost when done right: under 1 W.

## Item 21: the one measurement, and the plan at 3T

The single measurement that reduces the 3T-to-5T uncertainty most is row 11 followed by row 12: the register-only FMA chain at full occupancy for 30 s, then the same with one shared load and one shuffle per two FMAs, each with clocks.sm, power.draw, the live limit, the limiter reason and temperature at 100 ms, plus an nsys capture for the issued warp instruction rate. Two points give nJ per warp instruction against MIO share; K's mix (about 10% MIO) interpolates; the clock the limiter settles at in row 11 is the cap clock. That takes one worktree build of a 100-line NVRTC example and one 5-minute pause. The order after it: rows 0 to 3 (the host tax), then row 19 (the 10-minute thermal steady state), then the Radeon rows, then the settings rows.

If the calibration lands at 3T: at 2 substeps the whole system is 0.9 to 1.3M/s before the host tax and 0.7 to 1.2 after it. The full ladder at ml's rate adds 1.28x, the memory clock and profile rows at most 1.1x together; that reaches 1.0 to 1.6M/s. So yes, at 3T 2M/s is off the table on this laptop unless 1 substep passes its honesty bar, and even then it lands at 1.9 to 2.9M/s with the tax and the thermal row still to pay. The plan at 3T is to say so to the owner in one sentence, target 1.2M/s sustained as the shipped number, and put the remaining effort into the two things that move joules per creature-step at 3T: the substep ladder (physics's L1 to L3, since 1 substep on flight and ground-holding steps alone is worth part of the 1.85x at lower risk), and the MIO share of the kernel, which the same rows price. A desktop GPU is the other answer, and it is not a software plan.

## Item 11: persistent kernels against driver faults, CUDA errors and out-of-memory from agents

What exists today (scheduler.rs, recover): on any engine error the device is marked failed, the engine is dropped, the scheduler waits 1, 4 then 10 s, reopens the engine and resubmits every queued unit with its exact inputs, so the results are the same bits; after three failures the units go to the CPU engine, which is being deleted. The notices reach the event feed through take_notices.

What P changes: one persistent kernel per class owns the ring's block slots, the queue heads live in VRAM, and the genes and results live in pinned memory. Three failure classes and the path for each:

- A driver fault (an Xid) kills every context on the GPU, the game's and any agent's. The persistent kernel dies with its VRAM state. Recovery is the existing path if two rules hold. First, the gene arenas and the results ring are ordinary process memory registered with cuMemHostRegister, not cuMemAllocHost, so they survive the context; a context loss then loses only the device queues and the occupant table, both rebuilt from host state. Second, the host keeps its own record of which refs were absorbed (it does: the ring's flights) and treats every unabsorbed ref as not started; on reopen it re-registers the arenas, reloads the cubins from the disk cache (milliseconds), restarts the kernels and requeues from the first unabsorbed ref. Cost: 1 to 2 s of driver reopen plus the driver's own reset time after an Xid, which can be 5 to 10 s. Determinism: results are by index and the inputs are the same bits; the set of creatures live at a world change during those seconds differs, which the chair allows.
- A CUDA error in our own kernel (an illegal address, a launch failure) takes the context down the same way and takes the same path; the difference is that it repeats, so after the third reopen the game pauses with the error in the feed instead of retrying forever. With no CPU engine, "pause with a message" replaces the CPU fallback, and that is the right product behaviour.
- Out-of-memory. P's VRAM is under 1 GB and allocated at start and at a class re-split, so the game's own allocations fail only at those two points, where the existing retry-smaller path applies. An agent's process that runs out of memory fails in the agent, not in the game; the driver does not evict our allocations. An agent's kernel beside ours time-slices with ours (Ada preempts at instruction granularity), so the game's rate halves while the agent runs and recovers when it stops; the persistent kernel is not killed by it. The owner's rule survives, with one caveat the game should print: when nvidia-smi shows another compute process, the rate shown is shared.

What the game does during the 1 to 10 s of a restart: the worker keeps handling commands and publishing snapshots, the ring holds its blocks, the event feed shows the reopen notice, the rate display dips, the champion replay (recorded frames on the host) keeps playing. Nothing is lost except time. The gate: EVOLUTION_SIMULATE_GPU_LOSS extended to fire in the middle of a persistent kernel's life, run twice with one seed, identical per-generation statistics after the loss.

## Item 20: the failure mode that survives rayon at 14, SCHED_BATCH and the UI slice

Two survive, and neither is on the UI thread.

The first is the worker thread. Controls are read between passes; a pass absorbs one block, and at a generation boundary it also runs the fit, the 5,000 tells, the statistics row and the snapshot with island summaries. A 300 ms boundary means a button press waits 300 ms, and a save (archives only, but serialized on the worker) waits longer. No scheduler setting fixes that, because the thread is busy, not starved. The UI keeps drawing at 60 FPS through it (the snapshot mutex is held only for the swap), so the player sees a frozen control, not a dropped frame. The gate is the existing control-latency probe at p99 under 5 ms measured across generation boundaries, which forces the boundary work off the worker thread onto the pool in chunks that leave the worker free to read commands every 5 ms.

The second is swap. 3.2 GB of the machine is in swap right now with the game not running, and the ring plus browsers plus agent builds exceed what 30 GB holds comfortably. The UI thread's pages are idle between frames, so the kernel evicts them first, and a major fault on the UI thread costs 1 to 10 ms on an NVMe under build load; a burst of them is a 50 to 100 ms hitch that no slice or nice value can prevent. Detection: perf stat -e major-faults on the UI thread, and VmSwap of the game in /proc. The gate: zero major faults on the UI thread over a 10-minute run with the ring at its full depth. What has to change if it fails: the ring's RAM (P's 0.3 to 1 s ring already cuts it 5 to 9x from today's 786k), no agent builds while the owner plays, and the owner's swappiness; mlock is not the fix.

## Item 24: disk

Measured now: the root filesystem is at 99%, 7.3 GB free of 468 GB. There are 74 evolutionSimulator worktrees whose target directories hold 244 GB in total; 22 of them contain a built game binary, 16 of those from 09-29 and 6 from today. The swap file is another 8 GB. A seeded worktree costs 9 GB of target, so the 6 to 8 parallel tracks of the plan need 55 to 75 GB before the first one starts, and a full-LTO release build needs 2 to 3 GB of headroom on top.

What the plan needs from the owner before any track starts: remove every worktree whose branch is merged or abandoned (git worktree remove, then git branch -d), which by the timestamps is most of the 74; target at least 120 GB free. A rule for the plan: a track's worktree is removed the day its branch merges, the orchestrator does it as part of the merge, and no more than 10 worktrees exist at once. The build cache seeding (cp --reflink=auto) does not share blocks on ext4, so every seed is a full 9 GB copy; if the owner is willing, a shared CARGO_TARGET_DIR per track family would cut that, but the simple rule is enough.

## Item 23: glitch risk from my domain's changes, with the guard

- Clock pinning, memory clock lock, platform profile: speed only, no effect on any result. No gap.
- Genes in pinned mapped memory read at take-up: a torn read if the kernel takes a ref before the block's genes are fully written. Guard: the host writes the genes, issues a store fence (write-combined memory needs sfence), then writes the block's tail word; the kernel reads the tail with an acquire load before any gene. Gap: none if the fence is there; a missing fence is a determinism failure, not a glitch, and the two-runs-one-seed test catches it.
- Results in pinned host memory: a partial 80 B record visible before the block's completion word. Guard: __threadfence_system before the completion word, and the host reads the word with acquire. Same test.
- Blocking waits and burst pacing: timing only. No result changes.
- GPU recovery by resubmission: the same inputs give the same bits; the guard is results by index. The gap the chair allows: the set of live creatures at a world change.
- Agents' GPU work beside the game: timing only; same gap.
- Nothing in my domain touches a score, a ledger, a bar or an archive; the exploit guards (ledgers, confirmation, audit lane, first_generation, physics_audit) are untouched by it.
