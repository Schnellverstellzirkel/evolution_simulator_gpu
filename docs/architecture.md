# Architecture

`config` owns validated, serializable experiment parameters. `evolution` stores genomes in contiguous arenas and creates deterministic parallel candidate batches. `qd` owns behavioral descriptors, the MAP-Elites archive, emitter allocation, and diagonal CMA-ES state. `physics` holds the physics limits, node masses and the older CPU reference. `cpu_engine` is the AVX-512 CPU engine that also records replays. `vk_engine` runs the WGSL kernel (`shaders/physics_creature.wgsl`) on Vulkan, and `scheduler` hands work units to the RTX, the Radeon and the CPU engine. `environment` lists the undoable environment effects. `storage` owns generation stages, statistics, and atomic checkpoints. `worker` runs the experiment off the UI thread. `ui` renders controls, the behavior archive, and the creature scene on a separate wgpu device from compute. The CLI uses the same experiment model and GPU implementation.

## Physics

The timestep is 1/60 s with 2 bone projection passes and 1 velocity pass (`EVOLUTION_PHYSICS_RATE`, `EVOLUTION_BONE_PASSES` and `EVOLUTION_VELOCITY_PASSES` override them). A creature is a connected tree of rigid bones between nodes. Bones are at most 2 m long. A bone weighs `physics::limits().bone_density` (4 kg/m^2) times its length squared, split between its two nodes in `physics::body()`, which every engine uses for masses. Evolvable organs add mass to their bone's nodes. Node 0 is a heavy head.

Muscles connect two bones at normalized positions along them. A muscle only pulls: it drives while its target length shortens and goes slack while the target lengthens, plus a passive damping term. All muscles of a body share one tempo and differ in phase. Force is capped at 100 N, and the target length changes at most 24 m/s. Each muscle has an energy store (120 J) that drains with the work it does and recovers half its deficit per second; drive scales with the stored energy, so an exhausted muscle does nothing. Touchdown sensors can restart a muscle's rhythm when a chosen node lands.

Each step is position based. Forces are integrated into predicted positions. Ground contact is projected, then the bone passes restore bone lengths and the joint-limit pass keeps every joint inside its evolved range. A node resting on the ground counts as heavier by `1 + STANCE_GRIP * grip` in the bone and velocity passes, so a body pivots over planted feet instead of dragging them. A parent-first rebuild then sets every bone to its exact rest length, recenters the body by mass, and lifts the whole body if a node ended up below the floor. The lift is a position-only correction: it adds no upward speed. Velocities come from each node's actual movement over the step. Coulomb friction limits each node's sideways speed change by its grip times the ground push it received, including floor clamps inside the passes; the whole-body lift's normal impulse (body mass times lift) also lets the feet on the ground resist the body's slide. A velocity pass removes radial bone velocity and limits bone spin. Node speed is capped at 60 m/s with the removed momentum redistributed over the body.

The first 200 steps' worth of time (1.67 s) settles the pose without gravity or contact. Then the body is placed on the ground and the 60 s trial starts. Fitness is the horizontal distance of the center of mass, nothing else. A trial ends early, keeping the distance reached so far, when the head drops below its neck, when a joint is forced more than 0.5 rad past its range, or when the head's acceleration averaged over 0.1 s passes 8 g (`physics::HEAD_SHAKE_LIMIT`). Behavior metrics for the archive (ground contact, cadence, height, feet) are recorded alongside.

A creature that could enter an archive is checked again from a slightly perturbed pose at 4x the physics rate and solver passes, and keeps the worse distance. Before it enters the global archive that players browse, the best candidate for each cell also re-runs its standard trial on the CPU engine, the engine that records replays, and keeps the worst of all its trials. The replay (`cpu_engine::replay`) returns the recorded frames with the engine's own result, so a replay ends and scores exactly as the engine did.

The physics lives in three implementations that must agree: the GPU kernel (`shaders/physics_creature.wgsl`), the AVX-512 CPU engine (`src/cpu_engine.rs`), and the older CPU reference (`src/physics.rs`), which lacks joint limits and fatigue. `cargo test --release --test simulation -- --ignored` checks GPU against CPU on the workstation.

## GPU execution

Evaluation runs one creature per GPU lane (`shaders/physics_creature.wgsl`). Creatures are grouped by node capacity into exact buckets of 3–8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, and 64 nodes. Inside a bucket they are sorted by node and muscle count, so the 32 creatures of a warp run similar loop counts. Node positions, velocities, start-of-step positions, and a force/shape scratch array live in workgroup memory laid out as `[node][lane]`, so every per-creature node index is bank-conflict free. Muscles and bones are packed per 32-creature tile as `[item][field][lane]`, so each warp load reads one coalesced line. Per-node and per-bone constants stay in private arrays. Each muscle is evaluated once per step and its force is added to its distinct endpoint nodes in genome order, which reproduces the per-node sums of the earlier kernel. The kernel keeps the earlier expressions; results differ from the old workgroup kernel only by floating-point rounding (see `docs/performance-log.md`).

The kernel runs through a raw Vulkan compute engine (`src/vk_engine.rs`, built on ash, with WGSL compiled by naga). wgpu inserts a full barrier before every dispatch that reuses a read-write buffer, which serialized the independent buckets. The engine records every bucket's dispatch for a step range and then a single barrier, so trials split into 64-step dispatches cost nothing extra. Buffers live in device-local, host-mapped memory (ReBAR), and each device has two submission slots so one batch uploads while another runs. `EVOLUTION_BACKEND=wgpu` runs the same kernel through wgpu, and `EVOLUTION_KERNEL=workgroup` selects the older shared-memory kernel for comparisons. Node-trajectory validation still uses the older kernel.

`src/scheduler.rs` hands a generation's creatures to the evaluation devices in body-size order, in units of about one second of device time, and keeps up to two units queued per device. The worker thread never blocks on the GPU: it handles every queued command, submits work, and collects finished units every few milliseconds. `evaluated` remains the contiguous completed prefix that checkpoints record. Commands that change or save the experiment first collect queued results. The default device is the discrete GPU. `EVOLUTION_DEVICES=radeon` adds the integrated GPU (bodies up to 16 nodes); with the desktop rendered on that GPU it gained nothing, so it is off by default. Results depend slightly on which device evaluates a creature, so single-device runs are the reproducible configuration.

The UI renders on the GPU that GNOME's compositor uses (the udev-tagged primary GPU, otherwise the boot VGA device). On this laptop the panel is wired to the Radeon, so rendering there leaves the RTX 4060 entirely to evolution. `EVOLUTION_RENDER_GPU` overrides the choice. While evolving or playing back, the UI repaints at up to `EVOLUTION_UI_FPS` (default 60; other repaint requests can raise it to the display rate).

## Memory and history

CPU genomes are packed in arenas with per-creature offsets. Population assembly uses bounded chunks and contiguous concatenation; it does not retain a million heap-allocated creature objects. Evaluation creates only a bounded batch of padded GPU states. The archive grid uses three measured behavior descriptors: ground-contact fraction, observed gait cadence, and center-of-mass vertical oscillation. It has 192 possible cells; each stores its highest-fitness creature, emitter source, topology, protection period, and visit count. Novelty is the mean normalized distance to the five nearest archived behaviors. Local competition compares an elite's fitness with behaviors in adjacent cells; emitter parent selection uses those local scores, while novelty parents favor underexplored and behaviorally isolated cells.

The four emitters start at 30% diagonal CMA-ES over a fixed topology, 30% constructive morphology, 25% novelty, and 15% random immigrants. A UCB-style bandit adjusts allocations from archive discoveries and improvements while retaining a prior-based exploration floor. Novelty samples underexplored and behaviorally isolated niches; an emitter with five stagnant batches restarts from a different niche. Structural edits split a bone while remapping its muscle attachment points, duplicate a mirrored joint with a connected bone and motor, or shift an oscillator group. New morphologies are protected from replacement by another topology for three generations. No offspring is an unconditional exact clone.

Generation history stores archive percentiles, centimeter fitness bins, counts by body size, settings, emitter feedback, and three representative archive elites. It does not retain every generation's full population. Checkpoints preserve the current candidate population, archive, CMA states, emitter feedback, and exact completed-evaluation boundary. Older checkpoints are migrated by retaining their current population and evaluating it into a new archive. Evolution checks its estimated temporary-memory requirement before constructing offspring.

## State transitions

`Ready → Evaluating → Evaluated → Archived → Ready (next generation)`

Guided mode pauses at evaluation, archive update, and offspring creation. Continuous mode repeats them. New parameter sets apply before evaluating a new generation, so one generation never mixes environments or fitness criteria. Saved stage/progress determines which work resumes after loading.

## Background

The archive follows the MAP-Elites quality-diversity pattern: [Mouret and Clune (2015)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.04909). The multiple emitter types and reward-based allocation are inspired by [Multi-Emitter MAP-Elites](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.05352). The fixed-topology diagonal CMA-ES here is a small custom implementation, not the full CMA-ME algorithm. Three-generation morphology protection follows the broader innovation-protection motivation behind [NEAT](https://nn.cs.utexas.edu/downloads/papers/stanley.jair04.pdf), without NEAT's species model.
